<h2 id="reader-content-selected-problematic-conjunctions">Selected Problematic Conjunctions</h2>
<h3 id="reader-content-až-and-když">AŽ <em>and</em> KDYŽ</h3>
<p>Both až and když can be used in more than one context (see table below). Only when used as the equivalent of the English when (i.e. temporally) can they become a source of confusion for the learner. Až always points to the future, while když describes events in the past, or the present, most often repeatedly (for instance, <strong><em>Vždycky když ...</em></strong> . is the only way to translate <strong><em>always when</em></strong>...).</p>

<p>Both až and když are found in a number of idioms and proverbs. E.g., <strong><em>když už tak už</em></strong> (used as when we're doing it anyway it might better be worth it), <strong><em>až na to ...</em></strong> (except that ...), <strong><em>mám toho až až</em></strong>, (I'm fed up with it), <strong><em>to je až moc</em></strong> (it's a little bit too much). There's even <strong><em>až když</em></strong> which means <strong><em>only when</em></strong>. <strong><em>Když</em></strong> + future tense is the equivalent of English 1<sup>st</sup> conditional.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-kdy-and-když">KDY <em>and</em> KDYŽ</h3>
<p>Now it’s time to put <strong><em>když</em></strong> into opposition with <strong><em>kdy</em></strong>. The basic difference is similar to that of between který and co. They both mean when. But the basic usage of <strong><em>kdy</em></strong> is related to time, e.g. question word as in <strong><em>Kdy příjdeš?</em></strong> (When will you come?), or conjunction as in <strong><em>Řekněte mi, kdy se můžeme sejít.</em></strong> (Tell me when we can meet.) while <strong><em>když</em></strong> is basically used as a conjunction in relative clauses <strong><em>Když přišel, bylo to hotové.</em></strong> (When he came, it was done.)</p>

<td>When you come it will be done.</td>
</tr>
<tr>

<td>When it’s done you will get money.</td>

<p><strong><em>Když</em></strong> is also used in the following way. <strong><em>Čtu jen když je světlo.</em></strong> (I read only when there is light.) <strong><em>Když to jinak nejde, přijdu.</em></strong> (If that’s the only way, I will come.) <strong><em>Když se mě to nelíbí, tak si to nekoupím.</em></strong> (If I don’t like it, I won’t buy it.) <strong><em>Když přijde, všechno bude v pořádku.</em></strong> (If/when she comes, everything will be OK.) <strong><em>Co když se jim něco stane?</em></strong> (What if something happens to them?) <strong><em>Udělám to i když nemám čas.</em></strong> (I will do it even though I don’t have time.) <strong><em>Když ono je to tak hezké.</em></strong> (But it is so nice!).</p>
<p><strong><em>Kdy</em></strong> is also used in a few rather bookish idioms. <strong><em>Nemám kdy.</em></strong> (I don’t have time.) <strong><em>Na to není kdy.</em></strong> (There’s not time for that.) <strong><em>Bylo to horší než kdy dříve.</em></strong> (It was worse than ever before.)</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-který-and-že">KTERÝ and ŽE</h3>
<p>One that sometimes causes misunderstandings is the English conjunction <strong><em>that</em></strong> which actually serves a double purpose. Consider these English sentences: 1) <em>This is the man that I saw.</em> 2) a. <em>He said that he would do it. /</em> b. <em>He doesn't know that he owes me money. <strong>That</strong></em> in 1) further describes a person (or introduces a relative clause) while in 2/ it indicates a. reported speech and b. a reported fact. These two are easily distinguished, and in Czech they require a different word. 1) in Czech, would be <em>To je ten muž, kterého jsem viděl.</em> while 2) will be a. <em>Řekl, že to udělá.</em> b. <em>Neví, že mi dluží peníze. <strong>Který</strong></em> is really an adjective, and therefore it takes on the case of the noun in the main clause (in Czech grammars you will find it classified as a <em>relative pronoun</em>). <strong><em>Že</em></strong>, on the other hand, is a true conjunction, and it doesn't change at all. <strong>Note:</strong> Unlike <strong><em>that</em></strong> in English, both <strong><em>který</em></strong> and <strong><em>že</em></strong> are always preceded by a comma.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-ať-and-aby">AŤ and ABY</h3>
<p>The dreaded <em>aby</em> and <em>ať</em> are next. The indirect imperatives, or subjunctives. They are very similar to French subjunctives. <strong><em>Ať</em></strong> is followed by the he, she, or they form of the verb, while <strong><em>aby</em></strong> requires the past tense. It takes on the function of the auxiliary, and changes its form to <em>abych, abys, aby, abysme, abyste, aby</em> (in literary Czech the correct we-form is abychom). The difference in meaning between ať and aby is very slight . The sentences in the chart below should demonstrate.</p>
