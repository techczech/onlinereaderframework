<h1 id="reader-content-differences-between-standard-and-non-standard-czech">Differences between standard and non-standard Czech</h1>
<p>As is usual in most languages, the Czech colloquial language has different pronunciation, and specific words and expressions. Unlike most languages, in Czech, the colloquial language also uses consistently different endings. For instance, you learn <em>mladý</em> (young) and hear <em>mladej</em> on the street, or you see <em>Šumavský chléb</em> (Šumava bread) written on a shelf in the bakery but people ask for <em>Šumavskej chleba</em>. As you see the difference is mostly in vowels.</p>
<p>It is also noteworthy that the Moravian 'dialect' uses many of the endings of the written standard, although not consistently, leading many Moravians to claim that they speak “better Czech” than Bohemians.</p>
<h2 id="reader-content-which-czech-is-taught-to-foreigners">Which ‘Czech’ is taught to foreigners?</h2>
<p>As a learner you don't have to worry too much about it, but it is important to keep in mind that you may hear some endings pronounced differently from how you learned them.</p>
<p>Choosing the right form can be a hard decision for a teacher to make. The standard variant is more regular but also more complex. If you started speaking colloquial Czech with beginners' mistakes it would sound ridiculous. The solution usually is, to use a simplified standard Czech and make it more communicative by introducing elements from the colloquial as appropriate.</p>
<p>The trick is that the two 'languages' are not always mutually exclusive. For two words in one sentence uttered by a Czech in normal everyday communication, you may find one ending colloquial and one literary, and there are no rules for this. Therefore, teachers cannot simply resolve to teach one or the other because except for in writing they are rarely separated. On top of that, Czechs themselves sometimes quarrel which is which.</p>
<p>The hard thing is to tell when to use which ending. The terms colloquial and literary or spoken and written are not very accurate. The colloquial variant is never used in writing (except, paradoxically enough, in literature), or for official purposes on TV, radio announcements, speeches, advertisements etc. You would use it with your friends but it is also used in less formal public situations like lectures, speeches to small audiences, etc.</p>
<h2 id="reader-content-some-differences-between-standard-and-colloquial-czech-spisovná-a-nespisovná-čeština">Some differences between “standard” and “colloquial” Czech <em>spisovná a nespisovná čeština</em></h2>
<p>This is a very important feature of the Czech language. In reality, there are two language codes that are used for different occasions.</p>
<p>'Literary' and 'colloquial' are not the best terms to distinguish between the two varieties of present-day Czech, but they are the most commonly used ones. Better terms might be Standard Czech and Common Czech (used by H. Kučera in his The Phonology of Czech of 1961). <strong>Standard</strong> Czech is taught in schools, used in mass media, and most written documents, both official and unofficial. <strong><em>Common</em></strong> Czech is almost exclusively spoken. It is used in all personal communication, and also for semi-official purposes, including public speeches.</p>
<p>Although there are many differences in vocabulary, pronunciation, and syntax, the most pronounced disparity is in morphology. For some grammatical models there are two very different sets of endings. A short example is given below.</p>
<p>The main problem for a foreigner learning the two different codes is that the difference is often a matter of scale. Also, often in the spoken language standard Czech forms are mixed with common Czech ones.</p>

<p>The difference originated at the time of the National Revival, when the, then 200 years old, language of Bible Kralická was taken as the basis for standard Czech by the revivalists like Dobrovský. But in fact, even in the time of Blahoslav, many of these morphological forms had been out of common use. Czech was also developing parallel to this, mostly among the common people, and mostly out of the cities. This language was used for writing only by Catholic priests who were striving to be understood by their audience.</p>
