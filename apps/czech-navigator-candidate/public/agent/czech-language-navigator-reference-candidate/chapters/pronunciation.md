<h1 id="reader-content-pronunciation">Pronunciation</h1>
<h2 id="reader-content-pronunciation-of-individual-sounds">Pronunciation of individual sounds</h2>
<p>Czech prounciation is not particularly difficult. It is about as hard as it is to pronounce any language, if it does not happen to be the one you grew up with. This is because your muscles just find it hard to adjust to new things. It is true, however, that pronunciation is often the most difficult part for adult learners and adults very rarely achieve native-like accent.</p>
<p>It is still important to practice pronunciation when learning Czech because Czech native speakers tend to value correct pronunciation over grammar when interacting with non-native speakers. A student of Czech will have an easier time trying to converse with natives speakers when getting the basics of pronunciation right.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-vowels">Vowels</h3>
<p>There are a few peculiarities in Czech that tend to cause problems for English-speaking mouths. The most troublesome is the pronunciation of <strong>vowels</strong>.</p>
<p>1) no matter where they are in the word, Czech vowels never loose their full quality, and are always <strong>pronounced fully</strong> and unflaggingly.</p>
<p>2) each Czech vowel can be either <strong>short or long</strong>. Long vowels are exactly two times longer than their short brothers. It is very frequent that vowel length changes meaning of words significantly. For instance, <em>děla</em> (cannons) is differentiated from <em>dělá</em> (s/he is working) only by the long <em>á</em> in the latter, and there are even more embarrassing examples. The length of vowels is completely independent on the <strong>stress</strong>. The stress is always on the first syllable in all Czech words. It is a very frequent that a long vowel occurs in an unstressed position as in <em><strong>dě</strong>lá</em>. There are two diphthongs <em>ou, au</em> and <em>eu</em> that are pronounced fully, as well.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-consonants">Consonants</h3>
<p>Czech <strong>consonants</strong> are much less awkward. There are many that are the same as or very similar to those in English. There are five, however, that are not in English at all: <em>ch, ď, ť, ň, <strong>ř</strong></em></p>
<ul>
<li><p>of the above <strong>ř</strong> is the most feared one; it is famous for its quirky nature and soft gurgling sound; it is the sound that Czech children learn last (around 5 – 6 years), and a certain portion of the population, including former President Havel, never learn to do it quite right; except for its fame and difficulty (it has been claimed that Czechs invented it deliberately to make Czech harder to learn) <strong>ř</strong> has little importance for learning the language, and many Czechs will be disappointed if you learn it well, which you very likely will</p></li>
<li><p><strong>ch</strong> is the same as in Scottish loch but mispronouncing it will cause no disasterous misunderstandings;</p></li>
<li><p><strong>ď</strong>, <strong>ť</strong>, <strong>ň</strong>, on the other hand have no claims to fame but play a much more important role in the language because they often distinguish between different meanings of similar words</p></li>
</ul>
<p>There are even more issues with sounds which are in both English and Czech but are pronounced just a little differently in Czech. For instance, <em>k, p, t</em> don't have aspiration, so it will be difficult for you to distinguish them from <em>g, b, d</em> when they are the first sound in a word. Czech <em>r</em>'s are rolled very much like the Scottish <em>r</em>'s. Finally, unlike English, Czech <em>h</em> is voiced, which probably makes it the most difficult of Czech sounds for English speakers. Other than that, there's nothing in Czech that will surprise you.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-contrasts">Contrasts</h3>
<p>There are several groups of sounds which have something in common one way or another. <em>Vowels</em> and <em>consonants</em> are one example. Vowels are <em>long</em> and <em>short</em>. Consonants are <em>soft</em> and <em>hard</em>, or <em>voiced</em> and <em>unvoiced</em>. It is important to be aware of this opposition since very often when the form of a word changes the ending consonant changes from hard to soft. See the <em>how to</em> spell it charts below for examples.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-short-and-long-vowels">Short and long vowels</h4>
<p>There are many pairs of Czech words which are distinguished only by the length of the vowels.</p>

<p>The distinction between soft and hard consonants is very important for Czech.</p>
<p>One of the less important implications is the spelling of <em>i</em> and <em>y</em> which are pronounced the same. The <em>i</em> only follows soft consonants, and <em>y</em> hard.</p>
<p>But models of nouns and adjectives are also divided into hard and soft depending on the final consontant. Soft and hard models have different endings.</p>
<p>There is also a group of sounds that are neutral. Neither soft nor hard.</p>

<h4 id="reader-content-voiced-vs-unvoiced-voiceless">Voiced vs unvoiced (voiceless)</h4>
<p>Some consonants are <em>voiced</em> and some are <em>unvoiced</em> (or <em>voiceless</em>). In Czech, it is very important to be aware of the difference because all <em>voiced</em> consonants become <em>unvoiced</em> at the end of words. For instance, <em>muž</em> (man) is pronounced muš. The other rule is that there can only be two consonants of the same quality next to each other, and the second governs the first. For instance, <em>kdy</em> (when) is pronounced gdy, etc.</p>

<p>The following consonants do not have unvoiced counterparts, and therefore are not influenced by nor do they do anything to other consonants: <em>r, l, m, n, j</em>. So e.g. <em>z</em> in <em>znát</em> (to know) does not change to <em>s</em> as it would for instance in <em>zpátky</em> (back). Nor does <em>s</em> followed by <em>n</em> change to <em>z</em> as in <em>sníst</em> (to eat up) as it would in <em>les dole</em> (forest below) pronounced as <em>lezdole</em>. See section on Assimilation below for much more detail.</p>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> The last three contrasts only appear in cases of assimilation. The exception is the the loan word <em>džus</em> which forms a minimal pair with another loan word <em>čus</em>. There are other rare loan words where <em>dž</em> appears unassimilated such as <em>maharádža</em> but which do not form minimal pairs with other words.</p>
<p><strong>Note 2:</strong> <em>Ř</em> becomes voiceless when next to another voiceless consonant, e.g. it is pronounced voiceless in <em>tři</em> but voiced in <em>čtyři</em>.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-voicing-assimilation">Voicing assimilation</h3>
<p>There are a few things that happen to individual sounds when put together. Especially consonants are vulnerable. If two consonants meet, the second always makes the first change its voicedness.<a href="https://czechnavigator.dominiklukes.net/agent/czech-language-navigator-reference-candidate/chapters/complete-table-of-contents.md#reader-content-fn1" class="footnote-ref" id="reader-content-fnref1"><sup>1</sup></a> For instance, in <em>kde</em> the voiced d changes the unvoiced k so that it is pronounced as voiced g. The pause is unvoiced so ending consonants are always pronounced unvoiced. Additionally, the following word always forces the change on the final consonant of the preceding word. Voiced consonants without an unvoiced pair (i.e. <em>m, n, l, r, j</em>) and <em>v </em>do not usually trigger assimilation.</p>

<h4 id="reader-content-k-g-voicing"><em>k</em> > <em>g</em> voicing</h4>
<p>When <em>k </em>is followed by a voiced consonant, it assimilates to the voiced <em>g</em>. Because non-assimilated <em>g</em> is almost always associated with loan words, Czechs do not realize that this is happening and will claim that they in fact pronounce <em>k.</em></p>
<p>This voicing assimilation happens within as well as between words. It is most prominent in the very frequent wh- question words: <em>kde, kdo, kdy</em> which are pronounced <em>gde, gdo, gdy.</em></p>
<p>The final <em>k </em>in <em>jak</em> (how) is often voiced as <em>g</em> when followed by a voiced consonant. For instance, <em>jak do</em> (how to) is pronounced <em>jagdo</em>.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-consonant-clusters">Consonant Clusters</h3>
<p>Another prominent feature of Czech pronunciation is its tendency to cluster consonants. As many as 4 consonants can be in one syllable without any vowels. Thus, <em>zmrzlina</em> (icecream) is in fact a t-syllable word. Examples of monosyllabic words without any vowels are <em>smrt</em>, <em>krk, vlk, smrk, pštros</em>, <em>strom, stvol</em>.</p>
<p>6 consonants in a row can be found in verbs in the past tense such as <em>scvrkl</em> (shrank) forming two syllables and even a 8 consonant rarity could be contemplated in the verb <em>scvrnkls</em> (you flicked [sth] off a surface). There is also the famous Czech sentence <em>Strč prst skrz krk</em> which seemingly lacks vowels completely because <em>r</em> plays the role of the vowel.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-syllables">Syllables</h3>
<p>When you speak, it is not really the sounds you pronounce, it is <em>syllables, words and sentences</em>. Thus, when you practice pronunciation start putting the sounds into syllables and words. You will also find that then they are easier to pronounce.</p>
<p>All syllables have to have at least a vowel or a sound that plays the role of a vowel. In Czech, <em>r</em> and <em>l</em> can play the role of a vowel which is why it is possible to have Czech syllables with just consonants such as <em>krk</em> or <em>vlk</em>. It is possible to have have a syllable with just a vowel. In Czech, there are even two words <em>a</em> and <em>i</em> that are just a vowel. But it is not possible to have a syllable with just one consonant.</p>
<p>Each language has a typical syllable patterns, or combinations of vowels and consonants (VC). In Czech, these patterns are most typical.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-stress">Stress</h3>
<p>Syllables are what carries stress. In Czech, it is always the <strong>first syllable</strong> that is stressed. Each sentence is divided into stress segments.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>| <strong>Mám</strong> | <strong>rád</strong> | <strong>o</strong>vocné | <strong>kned</strong>líky. |</p>
<p>| <strong>Do</strong>brý | <strong>u</strong>čitel | <strong>má</strong> | <strong>rád</strong> | <strong>své</strong> | <strong>stu</strong>denty |</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Notice, that the <strong>first syllable</strong> (in bold) is always stressed even when the vowel is short and the vowel in the following syllable is long. The <strong>unstressed vowels</strong> maintain their quality and are not reduced. This is very different from English. In English, only the stresed vowel is pronounced fully, the others are reduced.</p>
<p>Also, the stress segments are of <strong>different lengths</strong>. In English, the space between two stressed syllables is usually the same. English is a stress-timed language which means that vowels in unstressed syllables are reduced to keep the space between stresses roughly the same. For example, “give me a call” becomes “gimmeuh call” or “I’m going to” becomes “amana”. That’s how knock knock jokes work. Czech is syllable timed and the space between stressed syllables is more variable. This gives it a different rhythm.</p>
<p>These two features make it harder (but not impossible) to rap in Czech. But most importantly, they make it very challenging for English speakers to sound natural. This is the <strong>most important feature of Czech</strong> <strong>pronunciation</strong> to learn well.</p>
<p>Usually, one word occupies one stress segment but sometimes words combine in a way that the stress shifts between them. Usually, prepositions and some particles like <em>mi, mu, si</em> or <em>se</em> are combined with another word to be in a stress segment.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>| <strong>Uči</strong>li se | <strong>čes</strong>ky| <strong>od</strong> rána | <strong>do</strong> noci. |</p>
<p>| <strong>Mě</strong>la jsem mu | <strong>rá</strong>no | <strong>za</strong>volat. |</p>
</blockquote>
