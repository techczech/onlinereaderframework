<h1 id="reader-content-word-order">Word Order</h1>
<p>Contrary to popular belief, Czech word order is not completely free. In Czech, word order serves to show what you want to stress. The general rule is <em>What you want to stress goes at the end of the sentence.</em> For instance, take the simplest possible sentence <em>Petr pije kolu.</em> (Petr is drinking coke.) The words in this sentence can be ordered in any way and it will still mean the same (unlike English where <em>Coke is drinking Peter</em> is a little awkward). BUT. Each of the orderings serves as an answer to a different question.</p>
<table id="reader-content-czech-table-169">




<tbody>
<tr>
<td align="left"><strong>Question</strong></td>
<td align="left"><strong>Answer</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"><strong>Co</strong> pije Petr? (What is Petr drinking?)</td>
<td align="left">Petr pije <strong>kolu</strong>.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"><strong>Kdo</strong> pije kolu? (Who is drinking coke?)</td>
<td align="left">Kolu pije <strong>Petr</strong>.</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Therefore, unlike English, from even a very simple answer you can guess what the question was. Notice that the answers are mirror images of their questions.</p>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> As you may see below the other possible combinations also make sense in Czech</p>
<table id="reader-content-czech-table-170">




<tbody>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p>Jí kolu Petr?</p></td>
<td align="left"><p><em>Petr kolu pije.</em> (No, Petr doesn't eat coke he drinks it)</p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p>Jí Petr kolu?</p></td>
<td align="left"><p><em>Kolu Petr pije.</em></p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"></td>
<td align="left"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p>Pije Petr kolu? (not mléko, or čaj)</p>
<p>Pije kolu Petr? (not Jana, or Karel)</p></td>
<td align="left"><p>(these two combinations would be most likely perceived as questions)</p></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Three words, thus, give us six possible combinations, and all have a slightly different meaning in Czech. The word order is always a little vague for even in English you have sentences like <em>see it he did</em>, <em>Petr he saw</em>, <em>Petr saw he</em>. To grasp the subtle differences you have to live with the language for a long time.</p>

<p>The auxiliary form of the verb <em>to be</em> (budu), can be anywhere in the sentence depending on the emphasis on. For example:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Zítra budu psát knihu. (Tomorrow, I will be writing a book).</p></li>
<li><p>Budu zítra psát knihu? (Will I be writing a book tomorrow?)</p></li>
<li><p>Zítra knihu psát budu. (I <strong>will</strong> [definitely] be writing a book tomorrow.)</p></li>
<li><p>Zítra budu knihu psát. (Writing is what I will be doing with the book tomorrow).</p></li>
</ul>

<h2 id="reader-content-reference-pronouns-anaphors">Reference Pronouns (Anaphors)</h2>
<p>No language likes to repeat nouns over and over. Instead, pronouns are used to refer back to nouns mentioned before. For example, instead of saying <em>He bought a new car. The car was expensive.</em> you say <em>He bought a new car. <strong>It</strong> was expensive.</em> You can also refer back to verb or whole sentences. For example, <em>I ran a mile under 10 minutes. <strong>It</strong></em> <em>was very difficult.</em></p>
<p>English uses many pronouns for this purpose: <em>he, she, it, him, her, his</em></p>
<p>There are several things to remember about Czech in this respect.</p>
<ul>
<li><p>The pronoun has to match the gender.</p></li>
<li><p>The pronoun may change according to case, especially after prepositions.</p></li>
<li><p>The pronoun doesn’t always have to be expressed.</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-relative-clauses-and-phrases">Relative Clauses and Phrases</h2>
<p>Relative clause or phrase is always begun with a relative particle. See <em>Conjunctions</em> for a list. The important thing to remember about the relative conjunctions (pronouns) <em>který</em> (which)<em>, kdo</em> (who)<em>, jaký</em> (what) is that they change with the case and gender of the noun they refer to. <em>Co</em> (what) can be used with any case without change.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-který">Který</h3>
<p><em>Který</em> can be translated into English as <em>who, what, that, which</em>. More often than not, English drops the relative pronoun, Czech never does. <em>Který</em> is always preceded by a comma.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-kdo">Kdo</h3>
<p><em>Kdo</em> (who) is used differently from English. It is common only in set phrases and only for <em>TEN</em> nouns. <em>Kdo</em> is always preceded by a comma. For other genders, <em>který</em> or <em>co</em> is used.</p>
