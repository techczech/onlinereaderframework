<h3 id="reader-content-pronouncing-js-at-the-beginning-of-words">Pronouncing <em>j</em>s at the beginning of words</h3>
<p><strong><em>Jsem</em></strong> is correctly pronounced as '<strong><em>sem</em></strong>'. Although, there are Czechs who will try to convince you otherwise. The same holds for words like <strong><em>jmenuju se</em></strong>. Note, however, that in negation, the <strong><em>j</em></strong> comes back. Therefore, <strong>nej</strong>sem and <strong>nej</strong>menuju se.</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-infinitive">Infinitive</h2>
<ul>
<li><p>The infinitive (the form listed in the dictionary) is mostly used with verbs like <em>chtít, muset, moct, potřebovat</em>.</p></li>
<li><p>It is the form of the verb that is found in the dictionaries.</p></li>
<li><p>The ending of the infinitive is always <strong><em>-t</em></strong>. Sometimes, especially in songs and poems, you may encounter the archaic form ending <strong><em>-ti</em></strong>.</p></li>
<li><p>The other exception is the verb <strong>can</strong> which has the alternative infinitive form <strong>mo<em>ci</em></strong> and verbs derived from it like <em>pomoci</em> (help). In spoken Czech, moct and pomoct more are common.</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-tense-and-aspect-saying-when-and-how-things-happen">Tense and Aspect (Saying When and How Things Happen)</h2>
<p>Traditionally, Czech is said to only have three tenses–past, present and future. All the complexity of how things happen in time is then expressed by aspect (vid). Traditional Czech is said to have two aspects–perfective and imperfective. The traditional <strong>definition</strong> is very simple: <strong>the perfective</strong> describes completed events happening once while <strong>the</strong> <strong>imperfective</strong> describes events in progress not yet completed. But in actual use things get much more complex not least because aspect is used slightly differently with different tenses. And this is one of the reasons why the this guide takes a different approach describing aspect from other grammars. It combines tense and aspect rather than keeping them separate. This approach has many advantages including the fact that it make the Czech tense system easy to contrast with English.</p>
<p>According to this new approach, instead of just three, there are actually <strong>eight tenses</strong> in Czech and each expresses a number of functions. To form these tenses appropriately it is necessary to use the verb of the appropriate aspect. There are three aspects of verbs – perfective, imperfective and repetitive. For almost all verbs the repetitive form is the same as the imperfective. Verbs of motion have all three forms.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-what-do-the-tenses-express">What do the tenses express</h3>
<p>Each tense has several functions, that is, it expresses a slightly different meaning depending on context. While no Czech tense has a clear equivalent in another English tense, most functions do. Sometimes there are two functions in Czech where English only has one and vice versa. For ease of use, functions that are not as common or are of more benefit to the more advanced student are marked [ADV]. This division is not solid but might provide an insight into which functions are more important to learn first.</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-aspect-of-verbs">Aspect of verbs</h2>
<p>This section describes some general facts about verbal aspect. The following sections describe in more detail its meaning and how it is formed. As we saw above, aspect only appears in conjunction with a tense (but there are some exceptions that make it useful to understand the principles of aspect on their own).</p>
<p>Here are some facts about aspect of verbs to give you more background:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Actions (running, reading, doing) and states (thinking, feeling) are described by verbs.</p></li>
<li><p>In Czech, each action can be described by one of three verbs: <em>perfective</em>, <em>imperfective</em> or <em>repetitive</em>. These are called aspects.</p></li>
<li><p>There are no neutral verbs in Czech. Each verb you find in the dictionary describes one of the three aspects.</p></li>
<li><p>The “aspect of a verb” determines how that verb describes an activity.</p></li>
<li><p>Each verb can only be used in one of the three tenses: past, present and future.</p></li>
<li><p>Because they describe the action as a whole which cannot all happen at the present point, perfective verbs cannot be used as a present tense.</p></li>
<li><p>The vast majority of <em>imperfective</em> verbs are also <em>repetitive</em>. Only verbs of motion have a special repetitive form.</p></li>
</ul>
<p>Here are some advanced facts about aspect:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Sometimes the aspect of the action changes even the meaning of the verb (sometimes slightly and sometimes beyond all recognition). For example, <em>číst</em> [read] and <em>přečíst</em> [finish reading], or <em>žít</em> [live] and <em>přežít</em> [survive]. Another example, where the completed action is described by two different verbs in English is <em>volit</em> [vote] and <em>zvolit</em> [elect].</p></li>
<li><p>If the perfective verb in the imperfective/perfective pair has a prefix, its meaning is likely to be slightly different. Some prefixes like <em>při-</em> change the meaning more radically, while some such as <em>u-</em> change it only slightly or not at all (cf. dělat/udělat).</p></li>
<li><p>If the imperfective verb in the imperfective/perfective pair has a prefix, the meaning of both verbs is likely to be fairly similar.</p></li>
<li><p>Some verbs occur in one aspect only. Some states, feelings and sensations only have the imperfective aspect. (<em>Chtít, být, mít</em> are some of the examples.)</p></li>
<li><p>Very few verbs (none of the common ones and mostly of foreign origin) describe all three aspects. For example, both <em>graduovat</em> [graduate] and <em>definovat</em> [define] can be used in all three aspects.</p></li>
<li><p>The aspect of verbs shows up even in nouns and adjectives (quite a few – nearly all derived from verbs such as <em>the thinking of this man</em>, <em>the thinking man</em>).</p></li>
<li><p>Some verbs don’t have a true pair. Such as <em>sedět</em> [sit] seems to have its perfective pair in <em>sednout si</em> but <em>sednout si</em> has its own imperfective/repetitive pair in <em>sedat si</em>. The latter two describe the process of sitting down, while the <em>sedět</em> describes the process of being seated.</p></li>
<li><p>Some verbs form a false pair. They describe different aspects of the same action but grammatically they are separate and often have another pair. For example, <em>hledat</em> [search, look for] seems to be the imperfective aspect of <em>najít</em> [find] but it isn’t because the perfective pair of <em>hledat</em> is <em>vyhledat</em> [look up] and the imperfective pair of <em>najít</em> is <em>nacházet</em> (which is also the repetitive form).</p></li>
</ul>
<h3 id="reader-content-actions-and-verbs-meaning-of-aspect">Actions and verbs (Meaning of aspect)</h3>
<p>Verbs describe actions. Actions generally have three parts. The onset, progress, and finish part.</p>
<p><img src="media/image3-wmf.png"></p>
<p>Czech verbs pick different aspects of actions, and are therefore classified into three classes. So called aspects of verbs. They are:</p>

<tr>

<h3 id="reader-content-pairs-and-hubs-of-verbs">Pairs and Hubs of Verbs</h3>
<h4 id="reader-content-verbs-organized-in-pairs">Verbs organized in pairs</h4>
<p>Most Czech verbs have at least one counterpart in a different aspect. Since the imperfective verb is almost always used as the repetitive as well, most verbs can be said to occur in pairs of <em>perfective</em> and <em>imperfective</em> verbs. A perfect example of such a pair is <em>vzít</em> vs. <em>brát</em>. <em>Vzít</em> describes the the act of taking as a whole while <em>brát</em> describes its process or repetition. But many pairs do not describe exactly the same meaning, because the nature of the completed action is different from its progress. See the list of top 150 verbs for some good examples.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-verbs-organized-in-hubs">Verbs organized in hubs</h4>
<p>Very often, an <em>imperfective</em> verb serves as the basis of a number of <em>perfective</em> verbs which are formed by adding a prefix. There are twenty prefixes available for this. So there may seem to be virtually twenty counterparts to any verb. But only the verbs of motion can take on all of the prefixes each. Often, one of the verbs in the hub has virtually the same meaning as the imperfective verb but and others have a different meaning. Very often they become separate verbs and find their own imperfective/repetitive verb to pair up with. These cannot really be said to be related to the original imperfective verb other than historically. The hub is in fact a collection of pairs.</p>
<p>Here is an example of a verb with a hub. The imperfective verb <em>psát</em> (write) has a true perfective pair in <em>napsat</em>. However, using prefixes, seven more verbs can be created from the original verb. All of these differ somewhat in meaning and some differ substantially (e.g. <em>podepsat</em> to sign). All of these non-true aspect pairs have found their own imperfective pair verb to be able to express the progress of the new meaning.</p>
<p><strong>Note 1:</strong> There is no verb <em>pisovat</em> nor is it possible to make <em>napisovat</em>.</p>
<p><strong>Note 2:</strong> Most of the prefixed perfectives can take on the reflexive <em>se</em> or <em>si</em> optionally. <em>Upsat se</em> can only appear with the <em>se</em>. <em>Dopisovat</em> can also occur with <em>si</em> in which case it means to <em>lead a correspondence</em>.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-differences-between-pairs-of-similar-prefixes">Differences between pairs of similar prefixes</h3>
<p>Describing these differences in words is cumbersome. It is best to compare meanings and try to internalize the difference for own use.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-u--vs.-do--as-prefixes-of-completion">u- vs. do- as prefixes of completion</h4>
<p>udělat (do once and completely) vs. dodělat (finish something previously unfinished)</p>
<p>ujít (complete a distance) vs. dojít (complete, finish walking, also idiom-run out)</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-od--vs.-roz--vs.-u--vs.-vy--vs.-na--vs.-do--vs.-z--as-prefixes-of-destruction">od- vs. roz- vs. u- vs. vy- vs. na- vs. do- vs. z- as prefixes of destruction</h4>
<p>odlomit (break a bit off), rozlomit (break into pieces[in half]), ulomit (break off a piece), vylomit (break out), nalomit (partially break off), dolomit (finish breaking off), zlomit (break irreparably [in half])</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-vy--vs.-roz--vs.-od--vs.-na--vs.-za--as-prefixes-of-beginning">vy- vs. roz- vs. od- vs. na- vs. za- as prefixes of beginning</h4>
<p>vyjít (set out), rozejít se (start walking), odejít (walk away from), našlápnout (step out)</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-po--vs.-popo--vs.-na--vs.-u--as-prefixes-of-degree">po- vs. popo- vs. na- vs. u- as prefixes of degree</h4>
<p>poskočit (jump a little), poposkočit (jump very little), nalomit (break a little), ulomit (break off a little piece)</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-future-tenses">Future tenses</h3>
<h4 id="reader-content-perfective-future-such-as-udělat">Perfective future – such as <em>udělat</em></h4>
<p>The formation of this tense is perfectly simple (or depending on your perspective really difficult). All that needs to be done is to conjugate a <em>perfective</em> verb (see conjugation rules earlier). If you find conjugation difficult, then you’ll find forming the perfective future difficult as well.</p>

<h4 id="reader-content-irregular-future-tense-without-budu">Irregular future tense without <em>budu</em></h4>
<p class="heading" id="reader-content-verb-být-to-be">Verb být (to be)</p>
<p>The verb <em>být</em> (to be) uses its future form only. The auxiliary budu is not used again.</p>
<p>budu být <strong>></strong> budu, budeš …</p>
<p class="heading" id="reader-content-verbs-of-motion">Verbs of motion</p>
<p>Verbs of motion form irregular future tense. For example, it is impossible to say <em>budu jet</em> and the form <em>pojedu</em> must be used instead. There is no infinitive <em>pojet</em>, however.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>jít <strong>></strong> půjdu, půjdeš, … (to go)</p>
<p><em>jet</em> <strong>></strong> <em>pojedu, pojedeš,</em> … (to go [by vehicle])</p>
<p>letět <strong>></strong> poletím, poletíš, … (to fly)</p>
<p><em>běžet</em> <strong>></strong> <em>poběžím, poběžíš,</em> … (to run)</p>
<p>lézt <strong>></strong> polezu, polezeš, … (to crawl)</p>
</blockquote>
<p>There are around 20 more or less obvious verbs of motion. See Verbs of Motion below.</p>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> <em>po-</em> also functions as an ordinary perfectivizing verbal prefix (see below).</p>
<p class="heading" id="reader-content-marginal-verbs-of-motion">Marginal verbs of motion:</p>
<p>There are a few verbs that only behave as verbs of motion in the irregular future tense:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>růst <strong>></strong> porostu, porosteš, … (to grow)</p>
<p>kvést <strong>></strong> pokvetu, pokveteš, … (to bloom)</p>
<p>trvat <strong>></strong> potrvám, potrváš, … (to last, take time)</p>
</blockquote>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> The imperfective future tense can also be formed with <em>budu</em> for these verbs.</p>
<p class="heading" id="reader-content-verbs-of-perception">Verbs of perception</p>
<p>Some other verbs (mostly related to perception) do not form the <em>budu</em> future tense. E.g. <em>vidět</em> (to see) where the future of <em>vidím</em> is <em>uvidím.</em> (<em>Budu vidět</em> is also possible but it means <em>I will be able to see</em>). The same holds for <em>slyšet</em> (to hear) and <em>cítit</em> (to feel, to sense, to smell).</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-voice">Voice</h2>
<p>The category of <em>voice</em> distinguishes between the <em>active</em> and <em>passive</em>. While in English, this is a very important distinction, in Czech it is not used as often, and the few passive verbs that are used more frequently you learned as adjectives. The meaning of passive as in <em>It was decided</em>. is usually expressed by the reflexive <em>se</em> in Czech (<em>Rozhodlo se.</em>). See <em>Uses of SE</em>.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-passive">Passive</h3>
<p>The passive form is very simple in Czech. It is rarely used in its true form. It is rather transformed into an adjective (see below).</p>
<p>The key to formation of the passive is the past form from which the <strong><em>–l</em></strong> is stripped and replaced with <strong><em>–n</em></strong>. If the last consonant is preceded with an <em>–a-</em> it is lengthened to <em>–á-</em>. Words ending in <em>– nul</em> are changed to <em>–nut</em>.</p>
<p>Passive is gender specific which means that <strong><em>–a, -o</em></strong> are used to mark the gender of the subjects.</p>
<p>The syntax of the passive is similar to English. It is used after the copula and the agent is expressed by <em>instrumental (Kým?Čím?)</em> as in <em>Jěžíš byl ukřižován Židy.</em> (Jesus was crucified by the Jews.)</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-mood">Mood</h2>
<p>There are three <em>moods</em> a Czech verb can be in. <em>Indicative –</em> simple statements; <em>Conditional</em> – statements of what <em>would be</em>; <em>Imperative</em> – direct commands. Unlike English, Czech uses very different forms of verbs to express commands.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-imperative">Imperative</h3>
<p id="reader-content-if-you-want-someone-to-do-something-you-tell-them-to-do-it."><strong>If you want someone to do something you tell them to do it.</strong></p>
<p>For example:</p>
<p>They repeat, <strong>repeat</strong> too!!!!!! Opakuj<strong>í</strong>, <strong>opakuj</strong> taky!</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Opakuj<strong>í, opakujte</strong> taky!</p>
<p>They repeat, <strong>let’s repeat</strong> too!!! Opakuj<strong>í, opakujme</strong> taky!</p>
</blockquote>
<p>In English you just name the action, in Czech you give the verb a special form and you add <strong>–te</strong> to it if you want more than one person to do something, or <strong>–me</strong> if you want to say <em>Let’s …</em> .</p>
<p id="reader-content-to-do-it-with-the-right-forms"><strong>To do it with the right forms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><p>Take the third person plural and strip off the ending <strong>–ou</strong> or <strong>–í</strong></p></li>
<li><p>The consonants <em>d, t, a</em> are softened into <em>ď,ť, ň</em> (Exx. <em>jedou</em> <em>je<strong>ď</strong>!</em>)</p></li>
<li><p>The ending <strong>–aj</strong> changes into <strong>–ej</strong> (Exx. <em>dělaj</em>[í] <strong>></strong> <em>dělej!</em>)</p></li>
<li><p><strong>–e-</strong> verbs are divided into two classes because it would be difficult to pronounce some of them without adding <strong>–i,</strong> (Exx. <em>začni!</em> <em>jdou</em> <strong>></strong> <em>jdi!</em>)</p></li>
<li><p>-<strong>i</strong> can also be added to some other verbs like (Exx. <em>myslíš</em> <strong>></strong> <em>mysli!</em>)</p></li>
<li><p>To command more people ( plural ) just add <strong>–te</strong> to the singular form</p></li>
<li><p>The words ending in <strong>–i</strong> change from <strong>–ite</strong> to <strong>–ete</strong> (Exx. <em>jdi</em> <strong>></strong> <em>jděte!!</em>)</p></li>
<li><p>To say <em>Let us do something!</em> you use <strong>–me</strong> in place of <strong>–te</strong></p></li>
</ul>
<h4 id="reader-content-telling-someone-to-come-along-or-close-to-you">Telling someone to come along, or close to you</h4>
<blockquote>
<p>Come here!!! Pojď sem!!!</p>
<p>Come with me!!! Pojeď se mnou!!!</p>
</blockquote>
<p>To do this you use the prefix <strong>po-</strong>. <strong><em>po-</em></strong> can be used with verbs of motion such as, <em>pojď</em>, <em>pojeď</em>, <em>poběž</em> parallel to their regular form <em>jdi</em>, <em>jeď</em>, <em>běž</em>. It implies direction to[wards] (me – the speaker) or together with (me – the speaker).</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-reflexive-or-self-se-and-si-verbs">Reflexive or self (<em>se</em> and <em>si</em>) verbs</h2>
<p>Some verbs only (or mostly) appear with one of the reflexive pronouns (<em>se</em> or <em>si</em>). Reflexives express a direction to the speaker. As in <em>I saw myself.</em> or <em>She gave this to herself.</em> In Czech, it is used even when the ‘reflexive’ meaning is hidden and becomes a part of the verb.</p>
<p>The <em>se</em> or <em>si</em> particles take up the Koho? Co? or Komu? Čemu? case slots which means that the following noun has to take a different form.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-se-verbs"><em>se</em> verbs</h3>
<p><em>se</em> reflexive verbs are never followed by a noun in the Koho? Co? form because <em>se</em> already fills that role. We can divide them by what follows:</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-true-reflexive-my-self">True reflexive (my self)</h4>
<p>You can add <em>se</em> to most verbs to express the idea of <em>self</em>.</p>
<p><strong>uhodit se</strong> to hit onself</p>
<p><strong>topit se / utopit se</strong> to drown</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-non-reflexive-se-verbs">Non-reflexive <em>se</em> verbs</h4>
<p>These verbs no longer have a very clear reflexive meaning but are always used with <em>se</em>.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-se-verbs-with-kdo-co"><em>se</em> verbs with <em>Kdo? Co?</em></h4>
<p><strong>jmenovat se</strong> to be called/named</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-se-verbs-with-koho-čeho"><em>se</em> verbs with <em>Koho? Čeho?</em></h4>
<p><strong>bát se</strong> to be afraid of something</p>
<p><strong>ptát se / zeptat se</strong> to ask [of] someone</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-se-verbs-with-komu-čemu"><em>se</em> verbs with <em>Komu? Čemu?</em></h4>
<p><strong>líbit se</strong> to be liked (lit. to be pleasurable to someone)</p>
<p><strong>nabídnout se</strong> to offer self to someone</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-se-verbs-with-kým-čím"><em>se</em> verbs with <em>Kým? Čím?</em></h4>
<p><strong>bavit se</strong> to have a good time; lit. to be amused by something</p>
<p><strong>zabývat se</strong> to research something</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-se-verbs-with-a-preposition"><em>se</em> verbs with a preposition</h4>
<p><strong>dívat se na / podívat se na</strong> to look at something</p>
<p><strong>líbat se s</strong> to kiss someone; lit. to kiss with someone . This is similar to make out with someone. <strong>Note</strong>: this is different from to give a kiss to someone which is <em>políbit někoho</em>.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-mutual-se">Mutual <em>se</em></h4>
<p><strong>potkat se</strong> to meet (each other)</p>
<p><strong>honit se</strong> to rush, to chase (each other)</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-se-passive"><em>se</em> passive</h4>
<p><strong>Říká se.</strong> They say (it is said)</p>
<p><strong>Rozbilo se to.</strong> It broke (it was broken)</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-si-verbs"><em>si</em> verbs</h3>
<p><em>si</em> reflexive verbs are never followed by a noun in the Komu? Čemu? form because <em>si</em> already fills that role. We can divide them into several types:</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-true-si-reflexive">True <em>si</em> reflexive</h4>
<p>These really express a direction to oneself.</p>
<p><strong>dát si</strong> to have (lit. to give to oneself)</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-non-reflexive-si-verbs">Non-reflexive <em>si</em> verbs</h4>
<p>These verbs are always used with <em>si</em> but don’t have a reflexive meaning anymore.</p>
<p><strong>číst si</strong> to read (lit. to read to oneself)</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-mutual-si-to-each-other">Mutual <em>si</em> (to each other)</h4>
<p><em>Si</em> is also used to express mutual meaning when two people or things give something to each other.</p>
<p>psát si to write to each other</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-casual-narrative-si">Casual (narrative) <em>si</em></h4>
<p>This is a very interesting use of <em>si</em> (a part of what is sometimes called ethical dative). You can add <em>si</em> to almost any verb to express the meaning of casualness <em>by your leave</em>, <em>as if nothing happened</em>.</p>
<p>My utíkáme a on si jde. We’re running and he’s walking (as if nothing happened).</p>
<h2 id="reader-content-verbs-of-motion-their-aspect-and-tense">Verbs of Motion – Their Aspect and Tense</h2>
<p>Verbs of motion form a separate category in Czech with respect to both tense and aspect. Although there are only roughly 15 of them, they are very important in Czech because, through the use of different prefixes, <strong>they appear at the core of more than 500 possible dictionary entries</strong>. They differ from the other verbs in two respects. 1) <strong>Future tense is formed with a prefix <em>po-</em>,</strong> and 2) they have <strong>a special form for the repetitive aspect</strong> (where other verbs use the imperfective form).</p>
<p>Also, some of them make a <strong>distinction between movement on foot and by a vehicle</strong> (as in <em>jít</em> vs. <em>jet</em> or <em>nést</em> vs. <em>vézt</em>).</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-how-to-use-the-verb-chart">How to use the verb chart</h3>
<h4 id="reader-content-verb">Verb</h4>
<p>The verb as it is found in the dictionary. Typically only one aspect is given for each pair. It is the one that seems to be more common (whenever possible).</p>
<p><strong>Note on <em>se</em></strong> <strong>and <em>si</em>:</strong> If <em>se</em> or <em>si</em> can be used with the verb, they are listed in parentheses. If they are not in parentheses, the verb can only be used with them. If used optionally, they typically change the meaning (at least to <em>self</em>) this is then given under English Translation also in parentheses.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-present">Present</h4>
<p>The conjugation is given in the forms of: first person singular (I), third person singular (he, she, it) and third person plural (they). When a verb has an irregular conjugation, these are the forms likely to be different.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-l-form-past">L-form (past)</h4>
<p>This is the form of the verb used in the past tense. <strong>–l</strong> means that the final <strong>–t</strong> of the infinitive is simple replaced with the <strong>–l</strong>. Otherwise the full <strong>–l</strong> form is given. Some verbs have more than one possible form. The more formal (written) is given first.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-n-form-passive">N-form (passive)</h4>
<p>Although the –<strong>n</strong> form is mostly regular, the full form is given for all verbs. Not all verbs can be used in a passive sentence but most need this form to make nouns and adjectives.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-imperative-1">Imperative</h4>
<p>Even though, the imperative is almost always regular, the full singular form is given for all verbs except for verbs ending in <strong>–ovat</strong> which only replace the infinitive ending with <strong>–uj</strong>. The plural form can always be formed according to the rules listed under Imperative.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-object">Object</h4>
<p>Sometimes called valence, this indicates what type of object is the verb likely to have. The question words indicate whether it is a noun, a noun following a preposition, or an adverbial phrase of time or place. The question word for the noun also indicates the case.</p>
<p>When two objects are separated by comma, it means, that one or the other can be chosen. When they are not, it means that both have to be present at once (although the exact word order may be different).</p>
<p>Objects listed in parentheses are optional. Those given without any marks, always need to be present in some form.</p>
<p>The slot marked by <em>koho</em> (for accusative) can be filled by <em>se</em> and <em>komu</em> (for dative) can be filled by <em>si</em> if one or both are marked as possible in the first column.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-aspect">Aspect</h4>
<p><strong>Ipf.</strong> marks an imperfective verb and <strong>Pf.</strong> marks a perfective verb.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-pair-same-meaning">Pair (same meaning)</h4>
<p>If the verb has a pair verb, with the same meaning but of the opposite aspect, it is listed here. <strong>Note:</strong> It may not be conjugated according to the same pattern. See Irregular verbs below to check whether it requires special treatment.</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-prefix-derivates-diff-meaning">Prefix Derivates (diff meaning)</h4>
<p>If there are verbs derived from this one by a prefix, the most important ones are listed here. They always have a slightly different meaning than the verb in the first column (which is not marked, however).</p>
<h4 id="reader-content-english-translation">English Translation</h4>
<p>The most straightforward English translation possible is given here. See a larger dictionary for more.</p>
