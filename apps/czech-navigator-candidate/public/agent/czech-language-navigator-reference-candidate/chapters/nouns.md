<h1 id="reader-content-nouns">Nouns</h1>
<h2 id="reader-content-gender">Gender</h2>
<h3 id="reader-content-what-is-gender">What is gender?</h3>
<p>All Czech nouns have what is called gender. The gender of the noun is most important when you need to decide on the form of numbers, pronouns, and adjectives describing it. The words describing or determing the noun must change their form to match the noun’s gender. It also happens with verbs in the past tense.</p>
<p>To match the noun’s gender, the other words have to either add an appropriate ending or changing an existing one. For example, <em>ten bílý dům byl</em> vs <em>ta bílá kniha byla -</em> that<sup>‑masculine</sup> white<sup>‑masculine</sup> house was<sup>‑feminine</sup> vs. that<sup>‑feminine</sup> white<sup>‑feminine</sup> book was<sup>‑feminine</sup>.</p>
<p>English only has natural gender (used for people and male and female animals) but nouns in most other European languages have gender. French, Spanish and German are some of the best known. In Czech, just like in these other languages, you for the most part have to learn which words belong to what gender by heart.</p>
<p>Czech has three genders (like German and unlike French or Spanish which only have two). They are <em>masculine</em>, <em>feminine</em>, and <em>neuter</em>. In some ways, you can think about gender as if, for Czechs every object was either a <em>man</em>, a <em>woman,</em> or a <em>child</em>. This doesn’t really have any real consequences, except, Czechs find it amusing when genders are mismatched.</p>
<p>For instance “juice, coke, wine” (<em>džus</em>, <em>kola</em>, <em>víno</em>) are all of a different gender (whereas in English they are all neuter). Thus, <em>dobrý džus, dobrá kola, dobré víno</em> mean respectively “good<sup>‑man</sup> juice, good<sup>‑woman</sup> coke, good<sup>‑child</sup> wine.” But Czechs find it funny when non-native speakers mix up the gender. <em>Ha, ha, ha,</em> dobrá džus (good<sup>‑woman</sup> juice), <em>imagine that</em>.</p>
<p>To make it easier to remember that gender mostly matters for the words that interact with the noun, in the charts in this collection, we use the gender forms for the word <em>this</em>: <strong>ten</strong>, <strong>ta</strong>, <strong>to</strong>. The sooner you start internalizing this connection, the better.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-how-to-tell-the-gender-of-a-noun">How to tell the gender of a noun</h3>
<p>In Czech, you can tell the gender from the ending about 80% of the time. The general rule is: <em>-C = masculine</em>, -<em>a = feminine</em>, and <em>-o = neuter</em> (the exceptions are <em>feminines</em> ending in a soft consonant or <em>e</em>, and <em>neuters</em> ending in <em>e</em> or <em>í</em>; there are also a few <em>masculines</em> that end in <em>a</em> or rarely <em>e</em>).</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-case">Case</h2>
<h3 id="reader-content-what-is-case">What is case?</h3>
<p>The word <em>case</em> refers to a set of endings that are used to indicate how words relate to one another. For instance, who does what to whom. In Czech, it is expressed through different endings. Nouns, pronouns, numerals and adjectives all use different case ending to express things like being an object (receiver) or a subject (initiator), or belonging to something. All prepositions also require that a noun has a case ending. Sometimes, the case ending tells you about whether you are somewhere or you are going somewhere. Sometimes a case ending helps link nouns together, e.g. if something belongs to something.</p>
<p>In the English sentence, <em>Petr has a son</em> the relationship between Petr and his son is set by the fact that the word <em>Petr</em> precedes the verb and the word <em>son</em> follows it. In Czech, this doesn't mean much, because Czech word order is more flexible. The relatioship between Petr and his some is captured by the case endings: <em>Petr_ má syna</em>. Thanks to the endings, you can also say <em>Syna má Petr</em> and the meaning is the same.</p>
<p>All verbs require the words that follow them to change form (that is add an ending). Most of the time, it is the object, but often there are other roles such as recipient or part of something.</p>
<p>In traditional grammar, cases have names such as <em>accusative</em> or <em>dative</em> that hint at the function. The names of the cases are not important, however. What really matters, is how the case is used in the language. What’s more, Czechs will mostly not be familiar with these names, they know the cases by their number. You can find out more in the final section on <em>Grammar as Czechs Know It</em>.</p>
<p>The following sections will describe the functions of the various cases and all the different rules for changing the forms of nouns, and other words.</p>

<p>The underlined words or endings have equivalent functions in both languages. All the cases, except the subject, can be used with prepositions. The 6<sup>th</sup> case <em>in/about ...</em> sth/sb is only used after a preposition.</p>
<p>You can ask for all the <em>italicized</em> words using question words <em>WHO?</em> and <em>WHAT?</em> <em>What</em> is it? <em>What</em> do you have? With <em>whom</em> will you come? In Czech, it is the same only there is a different form of <em>Who? What?</em> for each case. Therefore the cases are named by their Czech <em>Who? What?</em> questions, which is how Czech children learn them in school. The cases are also numbered. In English, they are known by their Latin names. You can see that summarized in the chart below. The cases are listed in the same order as in the examples above.</p>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> In most textbooks and grammar books you find 7 cases. But the seventh case is only used for addressing people, and does not express any relationship between things in the world. That’s why it is best to learn it separately. It is mostly confusing when listed with the other forms and that’s why it is left out of the charts and has its own section on addressing people.</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-xx-models">XX Models</h2>
<p>Different types of nouns and other words change their forms differently. Each group is named after a model word and it is also called a model (<em>vzor</em> in Czech). There are fifteen models of Czech nouns, including the more prominent sub-models. They are all listed in the chart below. Each model is given a common word to represent it. Different textbooks use different example words. Here we chose ones we hope are most useful for learners:</p>
<p><strong>TEN:</strong> <em>kamarád</em> (friend), <em>muž</em> (man), <em>čaj</em> (tea), <em>rohlík</em> (bread roll), <em>poradce</em> (advisor), <em>policista</em> (policeman)</p>
<p><strong>TA:</strong> <em>voda</em> (water), <em>rýže</em> (rice), <em>daň</em> (tax), <em>noc</em> (night)</p>
<p><strong>TO:</strong> <em>pivo</em> (beer), <em>kuře</em> (chicken), <em>nádraží</em> (station), <em>moře</em> (sea)</p>
<p>All models can have further submodels for some exceptions but we ignore these here. For example, <strong><em>rohlík</em></strong> has a common submodel <strong><em>les</em></strong> <em>(forest)</em> but we treat them as the same model. They only differ slightly in some cases.</p>
<p>Model and case are often used to mean the same thing. In fact, model describes which particular ending a word will take in a certain case. Once you know the gender, you can most often tell the model from the form in the <em>Kdo? Co?</em> case. But there are many words, where this is not possible (for example, feminine (TA) nouns ending in a consonant).</p>

<ul>
<li><p>Words in this case most often function as the subject of a sentence.</p></li>
<li><p>This form is found in the dictionary</p></li>
<li><p>This is the form used when naming or pointing at things (your name is…, this is…)</p></li>

<li><p>It is never found after a preposition.</p></li>
<li><p>In a neutral sentence, it is most often found before the verb but often comes after the verb when you want to stress the subject.</p></li>

<h2 id="reader-content-koho-co---accusative---4.-pád---4th-case">Koho? Co? - Accusative - 4. pád - 4<sup>th</sup> case</h2>
<h3 id="reader-content-function-1">Function</h3>
<ul>
<li><p><em>Koho? Co?</em> is the most important case of the Czech language but only the second most frequent</p></li>
<li><p>The Latin name suggests accusing. It describes the relationship of influence between two objects, people, or objects and people.</p></li>
<li><p>Its main function is the object of the sentence.</p></li>
<li><p>It is used with some prepositions (like <em>na</em>) to indicate directionality.</p></li>
</ul>

<h3 id="reader-content-most-common-verbs">Most common verbs</h3>
<p>This case is used as the object with most verbs that take an object. Here are the most common ones:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>mít</strong> (have), <strong>chtít</strong> (want), <strong>znát</strong> (know), <strong>vidět</strong> (see), <strong>vzít</strong> (take), <strong>dostat</strong> (receive), <strong>koupit</strong> (buy)</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Reflexive verbs with <strong>se</strong> such as <strong>bát se</strong> (to be afraid, to fear) cannot be followed by this case because <strong>se</strong> already is already in the accusative. The exception is <strong>učit se</strong> (to study/learn) which can have an object in the accusative in modern Czech although historically it required a dative.</p>
<p>Reflexive verbs with <strong>si</strong> such as <strong>brát si</strong> (take), <strong>umýt si</strong> (wash [one’s hands]), <strong>koupit si</strong> (buy) can require an accusative.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-most-common-prepositions">Most common prepositions</h3>
<p>This case is used after some prepositions for locations. The meaning is then typically that of movement or direction. It is frequently used in contrast with the Locative (Kom? Čem?) which signifies static location.</p>
<p><strong>na</strong> – <em>na diskoték<strong>u</strong></em> (to the disco) vs. <em>na diskotéce</em> (at the disco)</p>
<p><strong>před</strong> (in front of)<strong>, za</strong> (behind)<strong>, nad</strong> (above)<strong>, pod</strong> (beneath) – always meaning in the direction</p>
<p><strong>o</strong> – typically used with Locative but takes Accusative in the meaning of “bet something”, i.e. <em>vsadit se o stovku</em> (bet a thousand)</p>
<p><strong>v –</strong> typically used with Locative but takes Accusative in the meaning of “believe in”, i.e. <em>věřit v boha</em> (believe in God), Days of the week also take Accusative after <em>v</em>.</p>
<h2 id="reader-content-koho-čeho---genitive---2.-pád---2nd-case">Koho? Čeho? - Genitive - 2. pád - 2<sup>nd</sup> case</h2>
<h3 id="reader-content-function-2">Function</h3>
<ul>
<li><p>The core meaning of this case is <strong>belonging</strong>. It is equivalent to the English <strong><em>of</em></strong>.</p></li>
<li><p>It is used with many prepositions: <em>z, ze</em> (from), <em>bez</em> (without), <em>do</em> ( to), <em>od</em> (from), <em>blízko</em> (close to), <em>vedle</em> ( next to), <em>během</em> ( during), <em>kromě</em> ( except, besides), …</p></li>
<li><p>The partitive genitive is used after units of weights and measures and after other words denoting quantity.</p></li>
<li><p>deset deka sýr<strong>a</strong>, litr mlék<strong>a</strong>, kilo pomeranč<strong>ů</strong>, půl hodin<strong>y</strong>, málo cukr<strong>u</strong>, hodně káv<strong>y</strong>, trochu mlék<strong>a</strong>,… Kolik šunk<strong>y</strong> chcete?</p></li>
<li><p>In the plural, this case is used for counting with numbers of <strong>5 and above</strong> (see section on Numerals).</p></li>
<li><p>Because of its use in counting, this is the most frequent case.</p></li>
</ul>

<p class="heading" id="reader-content-singular">Singular</p>
<p>The model of <em>rohlík</em> has two alternative endings. You have to memorize which words use which ending.</p>
<p><em>Rohlík</em> nouns end in <strong>-a</strong> mostly in months.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>leden – ledn<strong>a</strong>, únor – únor<strong>a</strong>, březen – březn<strong>a</strong>, duben – dubn<strong>a</strong>, květen – květn<strong>a</strong>, červen – červn<strong>a</strong> (The only regular month is listopad – listopad<strong>u</strong>) and in place names ending in <strong>‑ýn/‑ín</strong> or <strong>–ov</strong>: Londýn – Londýn<strong>a</strong>, Berlín – Berlín<strong>a</strong>, Krumlov – Krumlov<strong>a</strong></p>

<blockquote>

<h2 id="reader-content-kým-čím---instrumental---7.-pád---7th-case">Kým? Čím? - Instrumental - 7. pád - 7<sup>th</sup> case</h2>
<h3 id="reader-content-function-5">Function</h3>
<ul>

<li><p>Also used to express permanent or acquired property similarly to Spanish ser and estar.</p></li>
<li><p>This is the least frequent of the Czech cases but it is still very important.</p></li>
</ul>

<h3 id="reader-content-special-use-after-být-as-acquired-property">Special use after <em>být</em> as acquired property</h3>
<p>The instrumental is also used after the verb <em>být</em> (to be) especially in the future to express becoming something. It is also used as the object of the verb <em>stát se</em> (to become).</p>
<blockquote>

<li><p>Means of transportation (travel by)</p></li>
<li><p>Condiments, side dishes (food with)</p></li>
<li><p>Personal names (being with)</p></li>
</ul>
<h2 id="reader-content-special-forms-for-talking-to-people-rules-for-changing-peoples-names-when-addressing-them-another-case">Special forms for talking to people: Rules for changing people's names when addressing them – another case?</h2>
<p><em>The first thing to remember:</em> in Czech, when you address someone you have to change the ending of their name. It really does make a lot of difference in establishing communication, even if you are speaking with the person in English. Expect that Czechs will change your name, too, when speaking to you in Czech.</p>
<p><em>The second thing to remember:</em> in polite conversation with someone older than you, or someone you don't know well always use their academic title rather than their name. Thus saying something like <em>Mr. Doctor</em>.</p>
<p>Traditionally, this special form is considered a case called <em>vocative</em>. Czechs know it as the 5<sup>th</sup> case (pátý pád). They all learn the rule as <em>Pátým pádem oslovujeme</em> (we use the fifth case to address).</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-informal-endearing-ways-to-address-someone">Informal, Endearing ways to address someone</h3>
<p>Czechs sometimes use a special form of the first name to address people. This is very informal and used only with the closest of friends in the context of endearment. Also, it does not work with surnames. This varies much from speaker to speaker. <em>Autobus</em> doesn’t belong to the model. If the noun ending in <em>–us</em> is a name such as <em>Spartakus</em>, <em>Ikarus</em>, etc., then regular <em>kamarád</em> endings replace the <em>–us</em>.</p>
<p><strong>‡</strong> <em>Album</em> only differs from <em>museum</em> in the plural. The difference is whether the <em>–um</em> is preceded by a vowel or a consonant.</p>
<h2 id="reader-content-most-useful-noun-endings">Most useful noun endings</h2>
<p>Learning all the endings of Czech nouns may seem daunting. However, most of them are only used quite rarely. You will need to learn them eventually but, in the meantime, you can get very far with just six including when no ending is added, which is also important. They are: -y/i, -a, -u, - Ø, -ů, -e/-ě and are listed roughly in order of frequency and importance.</p>
<p>As you can see, each of these endings can have multiple function and can be used different with different types of nouns. But if you ever need to guess how to change a noun, you will probably get much closer to the correct one with the top 3 on this list than any other. You will often sound better with the wrong ending than with no ending at all.</p>
