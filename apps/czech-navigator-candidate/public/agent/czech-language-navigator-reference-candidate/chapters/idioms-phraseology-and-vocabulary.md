<h1 id="reader-content-idioms-phraseology-and-vocabulary">Idioms, Phraseology and Vocabulary</h1>
<p>This section describes the very important parts of language that usually fall somewhere between the grammar, dictionary and the phrasebook. You rarely find them in one place. But without them, you can never consider yourself to be proficient in a language.</p>
<p>The list here is not exhaustive and is presented from the point of view of an English speaking learner. We focused on things that are different from English and a common point of confusion. However, they are real patterns and learners coming from any language can benefit from them.</p>
<p>This section is forever incomplete. There is a long list of subsections in progress that may appear in future editions.</p>
<h2 id="reader-content-words-with-a-difference">Words with a difference</h2>
<p>This section is particularly English centric. It describes words the usage of which is in some special way different from English. Speakers of other languages may find them unremarkable. These differences go both ways, often speakers of Czech will make mistakes in English because of confusions arising from these words.</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-ask-zeptat-se-vs.-požádat">ask: zeptat se vs. požádat</h3>
<p>There are two very distinct ways of asking. One is asking for information and the other is asking for a favor. This translates into two Czech words:</p>
<table id="reader-content-czech-table-216">




<tbody>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p><strong>zeptat se</strong></p></td>
<td align="left"><p><strong>požádat</strong></p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p>Zeptal se kolik je hodin.<br>
(<em>He asked what time it was.</em>)</p></td>
<td align="left"><p>Požádal ho, aby odešel.</p>
<p>(<em>He asked him to leave.</em>)</p></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> Often, <em>říct</em> [<em>tell to</em>] will be used instead of <em>požádat</em>. <em>They asked him to leave.</em> will be translated as <em>Řekli mu aby odešel</em>. [They told him to leave.]</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-jaký-vs.-který-what-vs.-which">Jaký vs. Který / What <em>vs.</em> Which</h3>

<tbody>
<tr>

<td align="left"><p>expresses quality independent of surroundings</p></td>
</tr>

<h3 id="reader-content-use-of-jejsou-is-vs.-there-is">Use of je/jsou – is vs. there is</h3>
<p>The Czech <em>je</em> has several uses that require slightly different translations into English. The translation may depend on word order or on the general meaning.</p>
<table id="reader-content-czech-table-231">




<tbody>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p><strong>je = is, jsou = are</strong></p></td>
<td align="left"><p><strong>je = there is, jsou = there are</strong></p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left"><p>*Herec je ve filmu.* = The actor is in a film.</p>
<p>*Děti jsou doma.* = The children are at home.</p></td>
<td align="left"><p>*Ve filmu je herec.* = There is an actor in the film.</p>
<p>*Doma jsou děti.* = There are children at home.</p></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> The same principle applies in the future (<em>there will be/bude</em>) and past (<em>there was/byl</em>).</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-ještě-still-vs.-už-already"><em>Ještě</em> – Still vs. <em>Už</em> – Already</h3>
<p>Although relatively simple, these two words can cause quite a few problems. Compare their usage in negative and positive sentences. Notice how the meaning of <em>už</em> changes from past to present. With the future tense <em>ještě</em> and <em>už</em> can substitute for the future perfect in English, as in <em>When you come, it will have been finished.</em> – <em>Až přijdete, už to bude hotové.</em> Unlike their English equivalents, both <em>ještě</em> and <em>už</em> always appear in the beginning of the sentence.</p>
<table id="reader-content-czech-table-232">




<tbody>
<tr>
<td align="left"><strong>Ještě – still</strong></td>
<td align="left"><strong>Už – already, finally</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left">Ještě jsem tady. – <em>I’m still here.</em></td>
<td align="left">Už jsem tady. – <em>I’m finally here.</em></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left">Ještě jsem tam byl. – <em>I was still there.</em></td>
<td align="left">Už jsem tam byl. – <em>I was already there.</em></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left">Ještě tam budu. – <em>I will still be there.</em></td>
<td align="left">Už tam budu. – <em>I will be there already.</em></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<h4 id="reader-content-like-things-and-people">Like things and people</h4>
<p>In Czech, there are two ways of expressing your likes. You can either like the way something looks <em>líbit se</em> or like it for its properties <em>mít rád</em>. <h3 id="reader-content-small-talk">Small talk</h3>
<p>There is no expression in Czech to describe the English ‘small talk’. There is a word for ‘chitchat’ (pokec) or you can say ‘Přijďte si popovídat’ (Come over for a chat). But there is no word for ‘small talk’. Sentences like ‘they engaged in meaningless small talk’ or ‘she is no good at small talk’ are almost impossible translate into Czech.</p>
<p>Czechs have many fewer tools to engaged in small talk. There is no equivalent of the ‘how are you’ although that may be changing under the influence of American TV. Some phrases you can use on meeting people casually without necessarily wanting to know how they are:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Jak se vede? (How is it going)</p></li>
<li><p>(Tak) jak to jde? (How is it going)</p></li>
<li><p>Jak se daří? (How is it going?)</p></li>
</ul>
<p>The response to these is something like:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Ale jde to. (Not bad)</p></li>
<li><p>No jo, to víte. Nestěžuju si. (You know. I don’t complaint.)</p></li>
</ul>
<p>However, these phrases are not quite as formalized as they are in English.</p>
<p>Asking about other people is also common:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>A co máma? (How about your mom?)</p></li>
<li><p>Jak se daří mámě?</p></li>
<li><p>Jak se vede bratrovi? (How is it going with your brother)</p></li>
</ul>
<p>A common noncommital answer is:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Ale jo, jde to. (Not bad.)</p></li>
<li><p>No to víte, jak by to šlo. (You know, how could it be)</p></li>
</ul>
<p>However, these are all rather informal and quite loose. A conversation can be built up from these blocks for a while.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-a-vs.-i-and-vs.-as-well">a vs. i / and vs. as well</h3>
<p>There is a significant differrence between <em>i</em> and <em>a</em>. 'i' means basically 'as well as' while 'a' means 'and'. Sometimes their meanings overlap slightly but there's always a subtle change in perspective.</p>
<table id="reader-content-czech-table-266">

</tr>
<tr>
<td align="left">Petr and Pavel were there.</td>
<td align="left">Petr and Pavel were both there.</td>
</tr>

<p>When <em>i</em> occurs before a noun, it means <em>even</em> or <em>as well</em>: 'I Petr tam byl' - 'Even Petr showed up'/'Petr was there, as well'</p>
<p>You can even have <em>a</em> and <em>i</em> as part of the same clause in spoken language: 'A i Petr tam byl' - 'And Petr was there, too.'</p>
<h3 id="reader-content-conditional-with-the-infinitive">Conditional with the infinitive</h3>
<p>Sometimes in colloquial speech, the first part of a conditional sentence can be expressed through the infinitive.</p>
<p><strong>Vědět to</strong>, nešel bych tam. If I had known it, I wouldn’t have gone there.</p>
<p><strong>Být vámi</strong>, nedělal bych to. If I were you, I wouldn’t do it.</p>

<h3 id="reader-content-dude-ty-vole">Dude! – ty vole</h3>
<p>Even though <em>vůl</em> (ox) is an insult, in its Vocative form, <em>vole</em>, it can also be used to mean something equivalent to <em>dude</em>. You can often hear people sprinkle their speech (when addressing a very good friend) with <em>ty vole</em>. This is not an insult but is also completely inappropriate for any context outside extremely informal interactions with friends. For example, it would not be used within the family context.</p>
<p>Some people use <em>vole</em> or <em>ty vole</em> as a filler in their informal speech similarly to the way people overuse <em>you know</em> or <em>you see</em> in English.</p>
