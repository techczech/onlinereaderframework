<h1 id="reader-content-spelling">Spelling</h1>
<p>Czech spelling is reputed to be very regular. It is true in that it is learnable. But it is not completely straightforward. The are basically three types of rules that are at work.</p>
<ol>
<li><p><strong>Letter = sound</strong>: a certain combination is always pronounced in a certain way. For instance, if you see <em>á</em> you pronounce it as aaaargh.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Letter combinations</strong> are pronounced in a certain way. For instance, ě in mě is pronounced differently than when it follows vě, or dě.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Assimilation of consonants</strong> described in the chapter on Pronunciation</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Exceptions:</strong> Some words or patterns of words are known to be spelled differently from the rules 1-3. Most common with some uses of <em>i</em> vs <em>y</em>.</p></li>
</ol>

<h2 id="reader-content-use-of-y-vs.-i">Use of <em>y</em> vs. <em>i</em></h2>
<p>The distinction between <strong><em>Y</em></strong> and. <strong><em>I</em></strong> is the most irregular phenomenon in the Czech spelling system. Both <strong><em>y</em></strong> and <strong><em>i</em></strong> denote identical sounds. <strong><em>Y</em></strong> is used regularly after all the hard consonants and never after soft consonants. Its use after ambiguous consonants is completely arbitrary and the words which use <strong><em>y</em></strong> have to be memorized by rote (even by native speakers.)</p>

<h2 id="reader-content-using-capital-letters">Using Capital Letters</h2>
<p>Czech rules for the use of capital letters are different from English. Titles of books and articles, and names of states and institutions all capitalize only the first word. Only cities with two names capitalize both. Also not capitalized are names of days of the week and months. Even words like names of nations are not capitalized when used as adjectives. Names of languages are also not capitalized.</p>
